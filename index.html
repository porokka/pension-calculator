What this model assumes
  • Today's rules for the whole career. Each working year uses the current contribution and accrual rates of the country you were in. Past rates were different, and Estonia's pillar II only started in 2002.
  • Your own figures. If you enter your Finnish pension record figure or your pillar II and III balances, they replace the modelled past and only the future is projected. The invested row and the whole-career comparison rows always use the model. Entered figures are kept in the page address so the link reproduces the scenario; nothing is sent to a server.
  • Your own investments. Today's value, plus anything you keep adding each month, grows at the investment return until you stop working and is then spread to the payout age like the other pots. It is added to your path, to the invested row and to the comparison rows alike. Tax on the gains is not deducted.
  • After a move. Each country pays its own share from its own retirement age, as EU rules require, and you work until the retirement age of the last country. The Finnish part keeps being revalued until it starts. Estonia's pillar I base amount is paid in proportion to the years worked in Estonia, a simplification of the EU pro-rata rule.
  • Finland. TyEL contribution 24.4 % of gross (you 7.30 %, the employer the rest). Accrual 1.5 % of each year's earnings, revalued 80 % by wages. The life expectancy coefficient and the retirement ages for people born after 1967 are approximations of ETK forecasts, not official values. The national pension top-up for low earners is left out.
  • Ministry of Finance proposal. Published in October 2026: the lowest retirement age rises half a year per birth cohort until 70, reached by those born in 1976. It is a civil-service proposal, not law. Ages for later cohorts are an extrapolation.
  • Estonia pillar I. Base 399.24 € plus 10.477 € per year of service (values from April 2026). Each year counts half as service and half by your wage relative to the average; in pillar II the wage half is reduced by a fifth. Both values are indexed here at 80 % of real wage growth. Retirement age after 65 rises about one month per birth year, an estimate.
  • Estonia pillars II and III. Pillar II gets your 2, 4 or 6 % plus 4 % from social tax. Pillar III contributions earn a 22 % income tax refund on up to 15 % of gross income, at most 6 000 € a year.
  • Belgium, statutory pension. Employee scheme at the single rate: each year adds 60 % of that year's salary divided by 45, with salary counted up to a ceiling of 82 609 € a year and at most 45 years. Past salaries are revalued by prices only, so there is no real growth on them. Retirement age 66, and 67 for those born from 1964. The household rate (75 %), the minimum pension, the planned bonus and malus and the solidarity deductions are left out. The 16.36 % contribution (7.5 % yours, 8.86 % employer) is the nominal pension share of Belgian social security, which is in practice pooled.
  • Belgium pillars II and III. Pillar II is whatever your employer pays into a group plan, entered as a share of salary. Pillar III is pension savings with a 30 % tax reduction on up to 1 050 € a year, or 25 % on the whole amount up to 1 350 €. The final tax on these pots at payout is not deducted.
  • The invested row. Finland: the full 24.4 %. Estonia: the 20 % pension part of social tax plus your pillar II and III contributions. Belgium: the 16.36 % plus pillar II and III contributions. Tax refunds are not included. This is a thought experiment. Pay-as-you-go contributions fund today's pensioners and cannot be redirected.
  • Not included. Income tax on pensions, market crashes near retirement, living past the payout age (state pensions keep paying, a spent pot does not), and the disability and survivor cover the statutory systems include.
An estimate for comparison, not financial advice and not an official pension forecast. For your real figures, check tyoelake.fi (Finland), pensionikeskus.ee (Estonia) and mypension.be (Belgium).
